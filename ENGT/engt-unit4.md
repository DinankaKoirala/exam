## Memo (Memorandum)

### What is a Memo?
- Derived from Latin *memorare* — "to remember" or "to remind"
- An internal communication tool (intra-office)
- Less formal than a letter
- Used to inform, instruct, inquire, report, suggest, or promote goodwill

### Purposes of a Memo
- To inform
- To inquire
- To report
- To give suggestions
- To remind
- To instruct
- To promote goodwill
- To communicate ideas

### Advantages of Memos
- Clear and concise — focuses on essential information
- Serves as a formal record for future reference
- Easy to distribute (email or physical copy)
- Cost-effective vs. organizing meetings
- Saves time
- Standardized format = easy to read
- Promotes accountability (specifies roles, tasks, deadlines)

### Memo Layout

**Heading Block:**
```
TO:      [Recipient's name and title]
FROM:    [Sender's name and title]
DATE:    [Full date]
SUBJECT: [Main idea in fewer than 10 words]
```

**Body Structure:**
1. **Context line** — Background or reference to a previous event/request
2. **Main message** — Points organized in descending order of importance (most important first)
   - Answer: What, Why, When (is it happening), How (it affects people), Who (is affected)
3. **Action close** — Clear call to action: what should be done, by whom, by when; where to go for more info
4. **Signature** — Memo is signed by the writer

## Letter and Email

### Email (Professional Context)
Email is the most common form of business communication today.

**Structure of a Professional Email:**
- Subject line (clear, specific)
- Salutation (Dear Mr./Ms. [Name],)
- Opening — state purpose immediately
- Body — organized, concise paragraphs
- Action close — what you need from the reader, or next steps
- Sign-off (Regards, Sincerely, Best,)
- Signature block (name, title, contact)

**Sample Exam Email Outline (from syllabus):**
> As an intern at ABC Consulting Pvt. Ltd., write an email to your Project Manager Mr. Ramesh about progress and difficulties.

Key elements to include using the outline words:
*Thank – challenging – progress – tight schedule – support – report – analytics – guidance – access – doubt – requirements – design*

Use formal tone, organized paragraphs, specific details.

## Job Application and Résumé

### Resume
- A **synopsis** (brief summary) of the most relevant professional experience for a specific job
- Usually **1–2 pages** maximum
- From French *résumé* meaning "summary"
- Used when **seeking a new job**

**What to Include in a Resume:**
- Name and contact information (phone, email, address)
- Current job/title (or desired position)
- 1–2 sentence personal summary
- Work experience (if any)
- Education and graduation dates
- Relevant skills
- Languages spoken
- Certifications relevant to the position

### CV (Curriculum Vitae)
- Latin for "course of life"
- **Detailed narrative** of work experience, achievements, publications, education, skills, awards
- More comprehensive than a resume
- Used for academic, executive, managerial, medical, and senior positions
- Accepted internationally; serves as a marketing tool for the individual

**What to Include in a CV:**
- Contact information
- Research objective / personal statement
- Education
- Academic appointments
- Publications (books, journal articles)
- Awards and honors
- Grants and fellowships
- Conferences
- Teaching and research experience
- Languages and skills
- Memberships
- References

### Bio-data
- Rarely used today; stands for "biographical data"
- Originally used in medical contexts (patient records)
- Emphasizes personal particulars: date of birth, religion, sex, race, nationality, address, marital status
- Used in some government institutions or grant applications
- Different from resume/CV: focuses on personal characteristics, not qualifications

### Key Differences

| Feature | Resume | CV | Bio-data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | 1–2 pages | No limit | Short |
| Focus | Relevant work experience | Complete academic/professional history | Personal particulars |
| Used for | Private-sector jobs | Academic, senior, medical posts | Government, grant applications |
| Detail level | Targeted, concise | Comprehensive | Biographical/personal |

## Internal Proposals

### What is a Proposal?
A formal document used to **persuade readers** to accept an idea, service, product, or solution.

A proposal explains:
- A **problem or need**
- **Suggested solutions**
- **Benefits** of the proposal
- **Goal:** convince the audience to take action

### Why Write a Proposal?
- To sell an idea persuasively
- To solve organizational problems
- To request approval, funding, or support

### Types of Proposals

**1. Internal Proposal**
- Written *within* an organization
- Sent to managers or company executives
- Examples: purchasing new equipment, changing insurance plans, employee software training

**2. External Proposal**
- Written for clients *outside* the company
- Used to sell products or services
- Examples: offering consulting services, selling software

**3. Requests for Proposals (RFPs)**
- A formal request asking companies to submit proposals
- Common users: government agencies, hospitals, city councils
- Examples: road repair projects, cybersecurity systems

### Proposal Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Boilerplate | Reusable template content |
| RFP | Request for Proposals |
| Solicited Proposal | Written in response to a request |
| Unsolicited Proposal | Written on your own initiative |

### Structure of an Internal Proposal
1. **Introduction / Problem Statement** — What is the issue?
2. **Proposed Solution** — What do you recommend?
3. **Benefits** — Why is this the right solution?
4. **Costs / Resources** — What will it take?
5. **Timeline** — When will it happen?
6. **Conclusion / Call to Action** — Request for approval

## Internal Reports

### What is a Report?
A specific form of writing organized around identifying and examining issues, events, or findings.

> "A report is a communication from someone who has information to someone who wants to use that information." — C. A. Brown

**Derived from French:** *rapporter* — "combination of related sentences"

### Types of Reports
- **Short/Informal Reports** — progress reports, trip reports, incident reports
- **Formal Reports** — longer, structured, with front matter (title page, table of contents, abstract)

### Criteria for a Good Report
- Clear purpose and defined scope
- Organized structure
- Accurate, factual information
- Concise language
- Appropriate format

### Common Report Sections
- Title / Subject
- Introduction / Background
- Findings / Body
- Conclusions
- Recommendations
- Appendices (if needed)

## Writing for E-Media

Writing for digital/electronic platforms (websites, blogs, social media, email) requires:

- **Scanability** — readers scan, not read linearly; use headers, bullets, short paragraphs
- **Conciseness** — even shorter than print; get to the point immediately
- **Active hyperlinks** — link to sources, related content
- **Accessible language** — simple, direct, no jargon for general audiences
- **Visual support** — images, infographics, embedded video
- **SEO considerations** — keywords help content be found
- **Mobile-first thinking** — content must work on small screens

## Sample Exam Questions (Unit IV Practice)

**Q:** Write an internal memo informing staff about a change in office hours.
→ Use TO/FROM/DATE/SUBJECT format, state the change clearly, give the action close.

**Q:** As an intern, write a professional email to your project manager about your progress.
→ Formal tone, structured paragraphs, use the outline keywords, sign off properly.

**Q:** What is the difference between a resume and a CV?
→ Resume: 1–2 pages, targeted, for job applications. CV: comprehensive, no page limit, for academic/senior roles.
