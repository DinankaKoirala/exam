### Q1: Four Goals of Technical Communication + Reporter's Questions vs. Mind Mapping

**Four Goals:**
1. **Clarity** — information must be immediately understandable
2. **Conciseness** — no unnecessary words
3. **Accuracy** — all facts and data must be correct
4. **Organization** — logical structure for the reader

*(Note: Ethics is sometimes listed as a 5th goal)*

**Reporter's Questions vs. Mind Mapping:**

| Feature | Reporter's Questions | Mind Mapping |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Structured (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) | Free-form, branching diagram |
| Best for | Factual, specific, focused topics | Brainstorming broad or complex topics |
| Process | Linear, guided | Non-linear, exploratory |
| Output | Specific factual answers | Network of connected ideas |

### Q2: Professional Email (as an Intern)

**Prompt:** As an intern at ABC Consulting Pvt. Ltd., write an email to your Project Manager Mr. Ramesh about your progress and difficulties. Sign as Ben.

**Outline:** Thank – challenging – progress – tight schedule – support – report – analytics – guidance – access – doubt – requirements – design

**Sample Email:**

```
Subject: Internship Progress Update — Week 3

Dear Mr. Ramesh,

I hope this message finds you well. I am writing to thank you for the 
opportunity to work on this project and to update you on my progress.

The past two weeks have been challenging but rewarding. I have made 
significant progress on the data analytics module and have completed 
the initial design documentation as required. However, given the tight 
schedule, I have encountered a few difficulties I would like to bring 
to your attention.

First, I have some doubts regarding the exact reporting requirements 
for the final deliverable — specifically, the format expected for the 
analytics section. Second, I have faced some difficulty with access to 
certain internal databases needed for the report. I would greatly 
appreciate your guidance on these points.

Your support in clarifying these requirements and facilitating the 
necessary access would help me proceed with greater confidence and 
efficiency.

I look forward to hearing from you.

Best regards,
Ben
[Intern, ABC Consulting Pvt. Ltd.]
```

**Key things graders look for:**
- Proper email structure (subject, salutation, body, sign-off)
- Formal tone
- All outline words used naturally
- Clear problem and request stated

### Q3: Motivational Poster — "You Are a Scientist!" (Huxley)

**Task:** Create a motivational poster for your classroom using a quote from "We Are All Scientists" by T. H. Huxley.

**Quote to use (paraphrased for poster use):**
> "The method of scientific investigation is nothing but the expression of the necessary mode of working of the human mind."

**Or:**
> "You have been acting on the principles of inductive and deductive philosophy during your whole life."

**Daily life activities that show students use science without realizing it:**
- Choosing what to eat based on past experience (induction)
- Predicting the weather based on cloud patterns (induction)
- Diagnosing why your phone battery drains fast (hypothesis formation)
- Deciding which route to take based on traffic patterns (cause and effect reasoning)
- Testing whether a recipe works by trying it (experimental verification)

**Poster Design Elements:**
- Title: "You Are a Scientist!"
- Central quote (from Huxley)
- 3–4 illustrated daily activities
- Motivational tagline: e.g., "Every day, you observe. You reason. You discover."

### Q4: Social Media Literacy vs. Digital Literacy

**Is being able to use social media the same as being digitally literate? Why or why not?**

**Answer: No. They are not the same.**

Social media literacy is a *subset* of digital literacy — it covers only one narrow slice of what digital literacy entails.

**What social media use involves:**
- Navigating platforms (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn)
- Posting, sharing, commenting
- Managing a profile and privacy settings
- Basic content consumption

**What digital literacy involves (beyond social media):**
1. **Critical evaluation of information** — distinguishing credible sources from misinformation
2. **Cybersecurity awareness** — protecting personal data, recognizing phishing
3. **Ethical online behavior** — respecting copyright, privacy, digital etiquette
4. **Content creation** — using digital tools to produce documents, designs, videos, code
5. **Data literacy** — reading and interpreting graphs, spreadsheets, analytics
6. **Tool proficiency** — word processors, design software, project management tools
7. **Understanding digital footprints** — knowing that all online activity leaves a trace

**Why this distinction matters:**
A person can scroll Instagram all day and still be digitally illiterate. Digital literacy requires *active, critical, and responsible* engagement — not just passive consumption. In professional and academic settings, digital literacy determines your ability to work effectively, ethically, and securely in a digital world.

## Quick-Reference: Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Technical Communication | Clear, concise, audience-centered communication about technical or specialized topics |
| Clarity | The quality of being immediately understandable |
| Active Voice | Subject performs the action (preferred in technical writing) |
| High-Tech Audience | Expert readers in your field |
| Lay Audience | Non-expert readers with no specialized knowledge |
| Reporter's Questions | Who, What, When, Where, Why, How — used to gather specific detail |
| Mind Mapping | Free-form brainstorming diagram showing idea connections |
| Memo | Internal organizational document; informal; from Latin "memorare" |
| Résumé | 1–2 page targeted summary of relevant experience for a job |
| CV | Comprehensive academic/professional history; no page limit |
| Proposal | Formal document persuading readers to accept an idea or solution |
| Internal Proposal | Proposal written within an organization, sent to management |
| Digital Literacy | Ability to use digital tools effectively, responsibly, and critically |
| Induction | Drawing a general conclusion from specific observations |
| Deduction | Applying a general rule to a specific case |
| Hypothesis | A tentative explanation formed from observations, subject to verification |
| Persuasion | Writing that appeals primarily to emotion to convince |
| Argument | Writing that appeals primarily to logic and reason |
| Ethos | Ethical appeal — credibility and trustworthiness of the author |
| Logos | Rational appeal — logic, evidence, data |
| Pathos | Emotional appeal — feelings, instincts, sympathies |
| Seminar | Formal educational/professional gathering for discussion and knowledge sharing |
| Visual Communication | Use of images, graphics, and design to convey information |
